Rousseau: The General Will

An in-depth analysis of Jean-Jacques Rousseau's core political concept, distinguishing it from the 'Will of All' and examining its implications for democratic theory.

Unit 2: Social Contract 15 Min Read

Introduction

Jean-Jacques Rousseau introduces the concept of the General Will (volonté générale) in his seminal work, The Social Contract (1762). It represents a collective intent that aims at the common good or common interest, standing in contrast to private interests.

"Each of us puts his person and all his power in common under the supreme direction of the general will, and, in our corporate capacity, we receive each member as an indivisible part of the whole."
— Jean-Jacques Rousseau, The Social Contract

Meaning of General Will

menu_book CORE DEFINITION

The General Will is the will of the citizens directed towards the common good. It is fundamentally distinct from the "Will of All" (the mere aggregate of private, individual self-interests). While the Will of All looks to private interest, the General Will looks solely to the common interest.

Key Features

  • check_circle
    Infallible: Rousseau argues the General Will is always right and always tends to the public advantage, even if the people themselves can be deceived.
  • check_circle
    Indivisible & Inalienable: Sovereignty, being the exercise of the general will, cannot be alienated or divided. This forms his critique of representative democracy.
  • check_circle
    Absolute: It has absolute power over all its members, guided by justice and reason.

Comparison: Social Contract Thinkers

Thinker Nature of Sovereign Key Concept
Hobbes Absolute Monarch (Leviathan) Security / Survival
Locke Limited Government Natural Rights (Property)
Rousseau The People (Popular Sovereignty) The General Will

Actions

lightbulb

lightbulb EXAM TIP

When discussing Rousseau, always clearly distinguish between the General Will (common good) and the Will of All (sum of private interests). Many students lose marks by conflating the two.
